The goal of this study is to compare the effectiveness of logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting in predicting the probability of borrower’s default. To achieve this, I first conducted a thorough analysis of the subject area, covering the theoretical foundations of credit risk, regulatory requirements, and existing approaches to credit scoring. Based on this analysis, I designed a comparative experiment on real world data. The dataset comes from the Lending Club and contains over one million records of consumer loans issued between 2007 and 2018. This large, publicly available dataset allows for a reproducible and practically relevant evaluation of the three models under fair and consistent conditions.
Let me briefly justify the choice of the topic.
First, probability of default models are a mandatory element of credit risk management in every bank, directly required by IFRS 9 and the Basel capital framework. This makes any improvement in their predictive power or transparency immediately valuable for the industry.
Second, despite the active adoption of machine learning techniques in banking, the number of open academic studies that apply these methods to real world data remains surprisingly limited. Most published works either rely on small or synthetic datasets, which weakens the practical relevance of the findings.
Third, the rapid development of explainable artificial intelligence tools, particularly SHAP and LIME, has now reached a level where complex models can be made sufficiently transparent to meet regulatory expectations. This creates a genuine opportunity to overcome the traditional trade off between accuracy and interpretability.
Fourth, the availability of the public Lending Club dataset with more than one million loan records allows us to conduct a fully reproducible study on real data that reflect current trends in consumer lending, rather than relying on proprietary information.
Finally, we adopt a rigorous comparison methodology: the industry standard Gini coefficient as the primary metric, combined with bootstrap confidence intervals, enables us to draw statistically sound conclusions about which algorithm truly outperforms the others.
These five factors together define the motivation and foundation of this graduation paper.

Now let me give a more detailed look at the data and the features we used.
The Lending Club dataset contains over two million approved personal loans issued between 2007 and 2018. For our analysis, we kept only closed contracts – either fully paid or charged off – and removed all records with missing values in the selected attributes. The final sample consists of roughly half a million observations.
The target variable is a binary indicator of default: one if the loan was charged off, zero if it was fully paid. 
The final set includes twenty three variables, which can be naturally grouped into four categories. First, the basic loan parameters: requested amount, term, and interest rate. Second, the borrower’s characteristics: home ownership, income verification status, stated loan purpose, log annual income, and debt to income ratio. Third, a broad range of credit history indicators: the average FICO score, credit history length, the number of delinquencies in the past two years, public records, recent credit inquiries, open and total accounts, mortgage accounts, collections, revolving balance and utilization, current delinquencies, the share of bankcard accounts above seventy five percent utilization, and charge offs within the last twelve months. Finally, the fourth group contains a single but extremely powerful feature: the ordinal sub grade assigned by the Lending Club, which already aggregates a wealth of credit information.

Let me now present the main empirical findings of the study. The cleaned dataset was divided into a training set containing seventy percent of the observations, a validation set with ten percent, and a hold out test set with the remaining twenty percent. The validation set was used to calibrate the predicted probabilities and to select the classification threshold that equalises precision and recall. The test set was never involved in training, calibration, or threshold selection, ensuring a completely unbiased evaluation.
All hyperparameters of the three models were tuned with a single objective in mind: to maximise the Gini coefficient on the validation data while preserving the ability of the model to generalise. This tuning was applied consistently across logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting.
The slide shows the resulting test set metrics with ninety five percent bootstrap confidence intervals. The first thing to notice is that precision and recall are perfectly balanced in every model. For logistic regression both metrics are about thirty nine percent, for random forest they are forty percent, and for gradient boosting they reach nearly forty one percent. This balance was achieved through the threshold optimisation procedure I mentioned earlier, and it guarantees that false positives and false negatives are treated equally.
Turning to the key indicator of discriminatory power, gradient boosting achieves a Gini coefficient of forty four point eight percent, random forest forty three point four percent, and logistic regression forty two point three percent. The confidence intervals for the Gini index are quite narrow and do not overlap across the models. This allows us to conclude that gradient boosting is statistically significantly better than both alternatives in its ability to rank borrowers by risk.
Importantly, this superiority in Gini was obtained without sacrificing the balance between precision and recall, and it is supported by rigorous bootstrap based uncertainty quantification. In summary, the results confirm that, under a fair and consistent experimental setup, gradient boosting delivers the best predictive performance for the probability of default estimation task..

The best performing model in our study is gradient boosting, implemented with XGBoost. It achieves a Gini coefficient of 45 percent, with perfectly balanced precision and recall at around 41 percent. The bootstrap confidence intervals confirm that this advantage over random forest and logistic regression is statistically significant at the 5 percent level.
To understand what drives the predictions, we applied SHAP analysis. The top four features are the ordinal sub grade assigned by the Lending Club, the loan term, the requested loan amount, and the debt to income ratio. The sub grade alone has an average absolute SHAP value of 0.39, far exceeding all other variables.
The direction of influence is economically intuitive: riskier sub grades, longer terms, larger loans, and higher debt burden all increase the predicted probability of default.
To sum up, these results are relevant both for academic research and for practical applications in banking, particularly for model validation and the development of next generation credit scoring systems
